Terms of Entry
New York City is often discussed with a focus on motion, speed, and activity, a characterization that could be attributed to the robust subway system, extensive congestion, and ambitious skyscrapers. While we do concede that the city is uniquely vibrant and stimulating, we find tremendous value in resisting its popular image, insisting on an understanding of New York through waiting, pause, and stillness. 
The city is not incidental to the argument of the exhibition, because the city has a history of operating as a site where the environment has been shaped politically. The decisions that guide its infrastructure, as well as its cultural institutions and public spaces, create a geography where documented decision-making consequently traces a progression of spatial hierarchies. Moreover, New York has spent over 100 years building upward and tunneling underground, lengthening how long it takes to get from one end of the island to the other. Here, speed equates to progress, with enormous political and financial resources being invested into a promise that no one productive should have to wait longer than necessary. Time is money; therefore, waiting is a waste. Pausing, in this sense, is outside of the city’s logic. To wait is to actively defy the priorities of a city epitomized by efficiency. Terms of Entry highlight the stakes that New York has made by directing so little attention to who has been left standing still.
As a discipline, architecture is approached with a focus on circulation and program. Waiting spaces do not fit neatly into these two dominant frameworks as they are characterized by their temporal function and lack of movement, mediating bodies until the official program begins. As a result, waiting as a program is treated similarly to spandrels (byproducts), disguised by the illusion of neutrality. This is a crucial misclassification. 
This exhibition explores waiting as a condition that is asymmetrically experienced in New York City along socioeconomic class and racial lines. How does the act of waiting inspire feelings of (dis)comfort? Because it is a primary condition of a city, waiting spaces become a determinant of where and who gets assigned worth. These spaces are architectural manifestations that make the relationship between the body and the institution legible, exposing whose time is found valuable and whose comfort is worthy of attention. 
The artifacts curated span performance art, photography, architectural drawings, art, and urban planning documents, offering a romance that exposes an untold history of New York that includes waiting and exclusion. We trace this story in stoops, lobbies, immigration centers, urban planning, designed exclusion/comfort, and prisons. This history is woven into the chairs housed in Guggenheim’s reception hall in 1946 to the tablecloth of “Shared Dining” (2015), a piece produced by ten incarcerated women who actively experience the violence of waiting. 
Our website displays our exhibition utilizing digital structures of waiting that are commonly experienced: the three dots, the buffering circle, the hourglass, the frozen screen, the skeleton screen, the spinning beach ball, the grey loading circle, the loading progress bar, the “please wait…” error sign, and the Google T. rex. These are icons derived from the contemporary vernacular of waiting. Along the top is a banner that includes phatic cues used to manage the experience of waiting “...LOADING YOUR EXPERIENCE. THIS WON’T TAKE LONG…” In doing so, we urge you to consider what feelings this inspires. Boredom. Impatience. Anxiety. Discomfort. Agitation. Time Distortion. Stillness. Hope? 
After waiting two minutes to enter the home page, the user is greeted with something resembling a waiting room. The user is reminded that waiting is inherently political and by strategic design. “YOUR WAIT TIME IS NOT AN ACCIDENT. SOMEONE DESIGNED THIS FOR SOMEONE ELSE.” The user is invited to take a seat. 
“QUEUE” presents our artifacts using the logics that inform the spatial conditions of waiting. The user has to navigate through the pieces in sequence, waiting for the text to gradually fade in, at which point the next artifact is cued. The user experiences the minimal mobility afforded in places of waiting, vulnerable to a predetermined, yet undisclosed schedule. 
The user can access our curatorial text as they navigate to “HOLD,” where we offer the written framework of our exhibition. "HOLD" is a space of pause where the user can engage with the analytical approach that informs the exhibition. 
Terms of Entry invites viewers to understand New York not just through its movement, but by paying attention to the spaces where movement is withheld. Through its collection of artifacts and the design of the website’s delayed navigation, the exhibition sheds light on the overlooked experience and impact of waiting spaces. The (dis)comfort they create is not incidental, but rather deliberate. To wait in New York is never simply to pause. It is to encounter a system that evaluates one’s worth through time, comfort, and access.

Artifacts & Descriptions



Eagle, Arnold. Boy on a Crumbling Stoop. Photograph. 1935. Museum of the City of New York, 43.131.11.154.

Arnold Eagle’s mid-1930s Lower East Side photographs, from *One Third of the Nation*, document New York slum conditions during the Depression. His series of images of people sitting on steps is powerful because Eagle shows poverty as a condition of waiting and public idleness. The tenement street begins to function as an extension of domestic space because private interiors are crowded and limited. The stoop reflects a scarcity not only of money, but also of privacy. Through this series of photographs, Eagle documents how domestic life unfolds for the working class under conditions of spatial constraint. The stillness of the figures also suggests exclusion from New York’s promise of speed and, by extension, prosperity.



Dorsett, Clyde. Free Waiting. Ink on paper (photocopy). Undated. Clyde Dorsett papers, 1940–1991, Box 17, Folder 6. Avery Drawings & Archives, Avery Architectural & Fine Arts Library, Columbia University.

Clyde Dorsett writes about the idea of “free waiting” as a design approach to institutional space. Dorsett does not see the waiting area as neutral, but instead emphasizes its importance by showing how it can shape a person’s emotional state upon entering a facility. In the piece, he describes waiting as something that can produce boredom, anxiety, and even a “deadening effect” when people are confined to narrow, controlled spaces where their only function is to remain available. His proposal for larger open areas, activity, reading material, and exhibits suggests that design plays an important role in shaping the experience of waiting, and that it can either deny or offer comfort and dignity.




Harrison & Abramovitz. Philharmonic Hall, Lincoln Center for the Performing Arts: Perspective Rendering of Lobby. Drafted by H. Blake-Smith. Undated. Max Abramovitz architectural records and papers, 1925–1990. Avery Drawings & Archives, Avery Architectural & Fine Arts Library, Columbia University.

This rendering presents the lobby of Philharmonic Hall not simply as a place to wait, but as a stage for elite public life. The building was part of a larger performing arts complex designed by Max Abramovitz for Lincoln Center’s original 1962 concert hall. The lobby’s polish and openness suggest that the people occupying it are not just delayed visitors, but socially significant patrons whose presence is itself part of the performance. Unlike an ordinary waiting room, which manages bodies through compression and control, the Philharmonic Hall lobby seems to provide room for visibility and circulation. The lobby is an architectural extension of Lincoln Center’s public image with prestige.




Riis, Jacob A. Children’s Playground on Ellis Island (roof garden). The Children of Detained or Waiting Immigrants. Photograph. ca. 1900. Museum of the City of New York, 90.13.3.99.

This photograph captures a waiting space designed for the children of immigrants held at Ellis Island while their families were being processed or detained. At first, the image gives the impression of freedom and openness because the children are outdoors, engaged in play rather than confined to an interior room. But that sense of relief is limited. On closer inspection, the playground is set on a rooftop, which suggests that mobility is still tightly restricted. 

The contrast between childhood activity and the larger condition of suspension surrounding it reveals how Ellis Island organized even leisure and waiting within the same logic as immigration control. Although the playground appears soft and humane, the children are still placed in a condition of pause because their families have not yet been allowed to move on. The photograph is therefore about more than children’s activity. It is also about the architecture of waiting at the nation’s threshold.




Lamb, Charles R., 1860-1942 (Charles Rollinson), and architects. “Planning Great Boulevards for the Future,” New York Times, August 9, 1908. August 9, 1908. Avery Architectural and Fine Arts Library, Columbia University; Avery Drawings & Archives; Charles R. Lamb architectural drawings and papers, 1897-1911. Avery Drawings & Archives. Avery Architectural &  Fine Arts Library. https://jstor.org/stable/community.35216392.

This New York Times cover, published on Sunday, August 9, 1908, includes an illustration of the diagonal avenues proposed by Charles R Lamb, Chairman of the Committee on Thoroughfares of the Municipal Art Society. This scheme of Manhattan's future includes wide avenues and highway infrastructure that cuts through the city, demonstrating the city's unrelenting promise to solve the problem of traffic and circulation. It is lower Manhattan that is designed around in an effort to improve efficiency---to address "the future problems of growth" and to "provide means for thousands of people to leave the skyscrapers more readily." This is accompanied by "curious benefits" like "Chinatown would be... perhaps destroyed." Urban planning thus reveals who is designed for, who is designed around, and who is designed against. The spans of highways gesture towards what is considered a problem to be solved and what can be ignored or exaggerated, implicitly producing assumptions around whose discomfort is to be resolved. 




Immigrants at Ellis Island, New York. Detroit Photographic Co., postcard, color. Seymour B. Durst Old York Library Collection, Box 1, Item 371. Avery Architectural & Fine Arts Library, Columbia University.

Waiting in line at Ellis Island meant participating in a threshold condition where immigrants were not yet inside or safe. This duration was determined entirely by forces outside of their control, such as quota systems, inspections, and perceived racial category. The postcard as a mass-produced and commercially distributed image allowed The Detroit Publishing Company to sell Ellis Island as a spectacle, in turn aestheticizing an experience of discomfort. The person who bought and mailed this postcard is not in those queues yet both the view and the consumer are reproducing the power dynamic of the immigration center. 





McKim, Mead & White. "General Waiting Room. 325 Feet Long. 110 Feet Wide and 150 Feet High. Concourse and Tracks. New York Terminal Station. Pennsylvania R. R. Company." April 1907. Photograph. Photographic Views of Works by McKim, Mead & White, item no. 155, AA712 M195 FF, Avery Architectural & Fine Arts Library, Columbia University. https://dx.doi.org/10.7916/d8-tzg4-tb02.
Waiting spaces function in two registers—the prospect and refuge theory, and the Panoptic condition. The general waiting room in New York's Penn Station is designed as an intermediary space between arrival and departure. The absence of walls and furniture in the great hall disrupts visitors' comfort, encouraging direct movement toward the assigned train terminal, a phenomenon theorized by prospect and refuge theory. On the institutional level, the general waiting room produces a panoptic condition of surveillance as the vast open floor plan makes visibility inevitable. The hall is a spectacular display of civic excellence, a priority that seems to be at odds with the bodily experience of occupying the space.

Bottom Right: Wright, Frank Lloyd (American architect, 1867-1959), and architects. Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum (New York, New York). 1943-1956. Avery Architectural and Fine Arts Library, Columbia University. https://jstor.org/stable/community.28523954.

The Upper East Side is part of what would become Museum Mile, and during this period was the cultural district of New York’s elite. In the same years, Black residents were being redlined out of this neighborhood. Exhibiting modernist art here in the 1940s and 50s was an act of cultural geography because Solomon R. Guggenheim was building an exhibition featuring specifically non-objective, abstract European work that required cultural literacy and paid admission to access.The drawing of the reception area and process designs of seating assume a visitor that already has the leisure to be there. The Guggenheim has also been widely criticized for the famously panopticon condition of the main rotunda, yet Wright’s sketches project the “comfortable visitor” as someone who is not hyper aware of their exposure in.a predominantly white, wealthy, cultural institution. They showcase a specific social experience of waiting that presupposes the participant has already attained the standards of the institution.













Signing of the IRT Subway Contract. Photograph, c. 1900. Digitized by Columbia University Libraries. HathiTrust Digital Library, hdl.handle.net/2027/nnc2.ark:/13960/t6pz7mq2s.

This photograph captures a room of suited white men signing a 35 million dollar contract to build New York City's first subway lines. In doing so, they determine the future of NYC's transit. Using tax dollars to build the subway station, it is critical to question which bodies this proposed plan is designed for. Those absent from the room—immigrants, people of color, working and middle class, and women—who built the physical infrastructure are, today, most dependent on public transportation. These men are responsible for determining where the line would pass through and which communities would be connected with one another. To live beyond the subway line is to determine whose mobility is less crucial. This line was the main artery that would connect the city from Brooklyn to Lower Manhattan, to Harlem, and the Bronx. It created nodes of connection and circulation to the city's major commercial, economic, and civic centers. Which connections are worth investment? How does this homogenous group of city planners determine whose movement is worthy of acceleration and who is left waiting? Is inequality inherent? 




Left: Piper, Adrian. My Calling Card #1. 1986. Printed card. Adrian Piper Research Archive Foundation, Berlin.

Right: Columbia University Class of 1877, Honoring James R. Priest. Photograph, 1877. Columbia University Archives, New York.

The calling card is a form that is historically associated with bourgeois social culture as a formal notice. Adrian Piper’s work repurposes this form to describe an anticipated encounter in which a racist remark is made in a place where one believed themselves to be among their own. The last line, “I regret any discomfort my presence is causing you […] ”,  presents the mannerisms of this space while simultaneously showcasing that the state of waiting for such a remark is perpetual. It is placed alongside a 1877 Columbia class photograph honoring James R. Priest, one of the university’s early Black graduates. Priest is surrounded by an institution that has “extended” his entry, and the photograph holds the question of what that belonging costs. 





Women of York Correctional Institution. Shared Dining, from Marking Time: Art in the Age of Mass Incarceration. 2015. Mixed media installation. Brooklyn Museum, New York. Published in Thompson, Nicole R., ed. Marking Time: Art in the Age of Mass Incarceration. New York: MoMA Publications, 2020.


Women of York: "Shared Dining" was produced by incarcerated women in York Correctional Institution. The table is a place of waiting, where you are seated and wait together for a shared dining experience. The installation comprises ten place settings that celebrate a woman each artist is inspired by.  Each place setting is composed using materials the artists would come across during their time in the correctional institution: plastic cutlery, paper plates, and styrofoam cups. Women of York is inspired by Judy Chicago's Dinner Party, a piece that recognized women's achievement and advancement. This piece recognizes the waiting voice through materials of confinement. This piece is now housed at the Brooklyn Museum; the women built, designed, and shared a table they cannot sit at.


Ross, Sarah. Archisuits. 2005–2006. Documentation photograph. Published in Sevtsuk, Andres, ed. The Arsenal of Exclusion & Inclusion. New York: Actar Publishers, 2017. Avery Architectural and Fine Arts Library, Columbia University, New York.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​

Hostile Architecture sets up purposefully uncomfortable structures across cities to prevent poor and unhoused populations from using them as resting points. The divided bench featured in Sarah Ross’ Antisuits is a design decision that positions armrests between seats to interrupt a lying body and make horizontal rest unavailable. The photograph makes clear that the design of public waiting infrastructure comes from city planning that has denied comfortable and accessible spaces to a wide range of bodies, while avoiding confrontation from law enforcement or signage. In a city where the right to pause in public is distributed unevenly across class and housing status, Ross reframes the simple act of lying down as a political proposition.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​
